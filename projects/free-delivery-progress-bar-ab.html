A/B test design: a free-delivery progress bar in quick commerce

This is an experiment design, not a results report. Baselines and sample sizes are shown as formulas and placeholders because I do not have the app's data and won't invent it.

Context

In quick commerce, small baskets are expensive to serve. Many apps use a free-delivery threshold. A common UI question: do users respond to a live progress bar ("Add Rs X more for free delivery") in the cart?

Hypothesis

If we show a progress bar toward free delivery in the cart and on product add, then average order value will rise among orders that start below the threshold, because the nudge makes the gap concrete and cheap to close.

Null: the bar has no effect on AOV among sub-threshold carts.

Variants

  • Control: current cart, delivery fee shown at checkout summary.
  • Treatment: persistent progress bar in the cart and mini-cart with remaining amount, plus 3 to 4 suggested low-cost add-ons that close the gap.

Population and randomisation

  • Unit: user, not session, to avoid the same person seeing both versions.
  • Eligibility: users with a cart below the threshold at any point. Exclude first-ever order (different behavior) and handle them as a separate experiment.
  • Split: 50/50, hashed on user ID, stable across the test.

Metrics

Primary: AOV among orders placed by eligible users (or revenue per eligible cart session, to avoid selection effects of only counting completed orders).

Secondary:

  • Share of carts that cross the threshold
  • Conversion rate cart to order
  • Items per order
  • Add-on attach rate

Guardrails (the real risk):

  • Contribution margin per order (a higher AOV with low-margin items can lose money)
  • Delivery cost per order (batching and slot impact)
  • Order cancellation and refund rate
  • Cart abandonment (progress bar could feel pushy)
  • App latency in the cart screen
  • Repeat order rate at 7 and 28 days (a nudge that hurts the next order is a bad trade)

Sample size and duration

For the primary conversion-type metric, per-arm sample size is approximately:

n = 2 * (z_alpha/2 + z_beta)^2 * p(1-p) / delta^2

For a continuous metric like AOV, replace p(1-p) with the variance of AOV, sigma^2.

Inputs I would pull from data: baseline mean and variance of AOV, minimum detectable effect that justifies the build cost, alpha 0.05, power 0.8.

Duration: at least two full weeks so every weekday appears twice and payday effects are visible. Do not stop on the first significant day.

Validity checks before reading results

  • Sample ratio mismatch: check the realised split against 50/50 with a chi-square test. A mismatch means the assignment or logging is broken.
  • Novelty effect: compare week 1 and week 2 effect. A fading effect means the lift is novelty.
  • Heavy-user skew: AOV has a long tail. Winsorise or use a log transform and report both.
  • Interference: if the bar changes pricing or inventory (add-ons selling out), note it.

Decision rule (written before the test)

Ship if primary rises with a lower confidence bound above zero and contribution margin per order does not fall and cancellations do not rise. If AOV rises but margin falls, do not ship; redesign add-on selection toward margin-positive items.

What I'd learn even if it fails

Whether the problem is awareness of the threshold, or willingness to add items at all. Next test would be the add-on suggestions alone.

Takeaway

The test is easy to run. The craft is in the guardrails: an AOV win that costs margin or repeat orders is a loss.
